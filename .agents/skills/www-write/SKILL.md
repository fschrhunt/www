# Give the page something to say

Read [the site brief](../../site.md) and the page being edited. For a note,
read the homepage too so the note does not accidentally become a second bio.

Find the actual material before finding the joke. What happened? What bothered
Fischer? What did he change his mind about? What can a reader learn here that
is absent from the landing page? Use supplied facts and existing work. When an
anecdote is essential and missing, ask for it; do not invent a personal history.

Write the useful version first, then find where the material is already funny.
A specific inconvenience, disproportionate effort, or quiet admission is often
enough. Keep the strongest lines and let the rest breathe. No joke quota.
Aim jokes at the situation or the author's own choices, not a confused visitor.

Compare the kind of edit, not just the words:

- Generic: “Welcome to my creative journey.” Better: begin with the particular
  thing this note is about. Skip the ceremony of announcing a website.
- Generic: “Powered by coffee and chaos.” Better, when supported by the work:
  “the icon is eleven pixels wide. somehow this took several conversations.”
- Unhelpful error: “you broke it.” Useful: explain what input needs changing.
  The error is allowed to be plain.

These examples illustrate decisions; do not paste them into each new page.
Read the draft aloud. Cut explanations after punchlines, inflated claims,
startup language, and sentences that could sit on anyone's portfolio unchanged.
Avoid manufacturing a neat moral at the end. End when the thought is finished.

Keep the surface's job clear. A product page needs a concrete use and an honest
status. A note needs its own observation. A button needs an understandable action.
Retain Fischer's accepted wording unless the task calls for editing it.

Deliver the requested copy or implement it in the requested page. Update metadata
and reading-time labels when the change warrants it. Do not rewrite neighboring
pages merely to make their tone uniform.
